The Last Lighthouse Keeper

When Meera Joshi arrived at the island in the autumn of 1987, the lighthouse had already been automated for three years. The government had sent her only to close the building and hand the keys to the harbour office. She expected to stay a week. She stayed eleven years.

On the first night, a storm rolled in from the west and the automatic lamp failed. Meera climbed the 112 stairs with a flashlight and found a corroded switch. She repaired it by hand and sat beside the lamp until sunrise, watching the beam sweep across the dark water. In the morning, a fisherman named Dattu Patil rowed to the shore to thank her. Three of his friends had been returning from the deep sea, and the light had guided them home.

After that night, Meera could not leave. She began to keep a logbook, writing down the weather, the ships that passed and the small repairs she made. Fishermen started leaving fresh bread and fish at her door. Children from the mainland visited on weekends, and she taught them to read the stars. The harbour office quietly stopped asking for the keys.

Years later, a new satellite system made the old light almost unnecessary, and officials proposed to shut it down permanently. The villagers gathered more than 2,000 signatures asking to keep it burning. Meera did not argue or make speeches. She simply opened her logbook and let the officials read the eleven years of entries. Many of them described boats that had been saved by the beam.

The lighthouse still stands today. Some people say it is useless in the age of technology. Meera believed that a machine can guide a ship, but only a person can care whether it arrives. That, she said, is the real work of a keeper: to be present, to be watching, and to make the dark a little less lonely.
